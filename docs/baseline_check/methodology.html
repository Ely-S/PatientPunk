Methodology and data · v0.3.1

Why these six questions

The corpus contains 243,289 posts and 3,602,691 comments from 15 Long COVID and ME/CFS communities on Reddit (2009–June 2026). The study used full-text searches and AI coding to identify candidate questions. Weekly Check v0.3.1 is a research draft awaiting patient validation. The coverage exercise assessed an earlier version, v0.2. See the study code and inputs.

Scoring

  • Report the four scores side by side. Don't add them into one number. Each one tracks a different thing patients say matters.
  • Average four weekly checks at the start and at the end of a study. A single week is noisy because these illnesses fluctuate.
  • Questions start blank. An unanswered question is missing data. The Q2 total appears once every group has a tick or a button pressed. Q4 and Q6 are optional. Clearing Q4 returns it to missing.
  • Candidate trial endpoint. The proposed win ratio compares changes in four-week averages for each treated–control pair. A difference of at least 1 crash day per week on Q1 decides the pair. Below that margin, the change in Q2 activities (0–23) decides it. This gives crash days priority at or above the provisional margin and allows an activity gain to decide a pair when the crash-day difference is smaller.
  • Responder definition for clinicians: a patient counts as improved if their Q2 count rises by 2 or more and their crash days do not rise by 1 or more a week (4-week averages). Both thresholds are provisional until the minimal important difference has been measured.
  • Report the 4 Q2 group scores too (at home 0–4, daily basics 0–6, getting out 0–6, full days 0–7). They show where on the severity range a change happened. A trial in mild-to-moderate patients could pre-specify the top two groups (13 items) as its primary; that cut is a proposal to test, not a validated threshold.
  • Q2 counts only activities done at least 2 days before the check, to give delayed crashes time to appear. This leaves a five-day activity window. With a fixed check day, the same two weekdays can be missed each week.
  • "None of these" is an answer (0); "All of these" ticks every item in its group; a blank group is missing data. The score shows a dash for a group nobody answered, so a skipped group is never scored as zero.
  • Q1 counts crash or flare days, whatever set them off. The optional "What seemed to cause them?" list is descriptive and not scored; analysts can separately count weeks whose crashes were set off by doing something (physical activity, mental effort, emotional stress or sensory overload).
  • "No earlier check" on Q5 is recorded as missing. It is the expected answer in the first week.
  • Record who supplied the answers. A patient may respond directly or have a carer read the questions and enter the patient's answers. Both are self-reports, with assistance recorded separately. A carer's own answers are proxy reports and should be analysed separately. The quiz starts a new set of answers when the source switches between patient and proxy.
  • Key secondaries: Q1 crash days, Q3 symptoms, Q4 feeling like yourself (optional). Q5 is the anchor for working out how big a change matters to patients. Q6 is read for content gaps and is not scored.

Supporting data

We searched Long COVID and ME/CFS forums for the words people use to describe changes in their health. AI reviewers examined selected posts to see which changes mattered and which an early draft of the questionnaire would miss. These findings shaped Weekly Check. The current questions still need to be tested with patients.

  • Crash days (Q1). A crash can start after the day that triggered it. The check asks how many days people spent in a crash or flare, whatever set it off. Crash-delay search
  • Activities (Q2). The checklist asks what people did without a later crash. The v0.2 review showed that the checklist needed more basic activities. Activity analysis
  • Symptoms (Q3). Reviewers expected the symptom question to pick up some changes the crash and activity questions would miss. Coverage exercise
  • Feeling like yourself (Q4). People sometimes described their health as a percentage of how they felt before getting sick. Many ratings clustered near the top of the scale, and some varied across posts by the same person. We made the question optional. Self-rating analysis

Reviewers most often chose a specific symptom (43%). They agreed on 12 of the 20 posts they both read (60%, kappa 0.46). Feeling normal, recovered or like oneself came up in 72 of 242 posts (30%), even though reviewers chose it as the main change in only 10%. We withdrew an earlier estimate based on keywords. People often described several changes in one post. Across the same 242 posts, reviewers found mentions of symptoms in 239 (99%), activity in 141 (58%), the effects of doing an activity in 81 (33%), crashes in 77 (32%), and fluctuations in 76 (31%). In 41 posts (17%), symptoms eased or held steady when people did less, or worsened when they did more. That is why the check asks about both symptoms and activity. Activities and their cost

We found 6,140 phrases about doing an activity without a crash or post-exertional malaise (PEM) across 3,845,980 posts and comments. Q2 asks which activities people did without a crash afterwards. The score also depends on what people had the chance to do and chose to try. Study source v02_analysis/patterns.py.

We compared authors whose posts mentioned an activity and who had a single severity rating. Authors in the severe group (5,753) were 5.0 times as likely as those in the mild or mostly functional group (2,782) to write that they could not sit up. The corresponding ratios were 2.6 for leaving the house, 2.4 for reading, and 2.1 for watching TV. Study source v02_analysis/milestones.py.

Average severity differed more between broad groups of activities, such as daily basics and work or exercise, than between individual activities with similar estimates. Many confidence intervals overlapped. We used a checklist spanning physical and cognitive activities so people can tick what they did without having to fit into a single level. Study source v02_analysis/ladder_ci.py.

How close people felt to their pre-illness selves

We searched for phrases that put current health at a percentage of how the person felt before illness. The search matched 339 of 5,062 posts in the recovery or improvement sample (6.7%) and 105 of 20,000 in the comparison sample (0.5%). In a separate check of 150 matches, AI reviewers found that 93 (62%) described the author’s current health. Study sources v02_analysis/domains_v2.py and v02_analysis/analyze_coded.py.

The search found 5,919 candidate ratings from 3,099 authors. Of those ratings, 55% were at least 90%. Among 764 pairs of consecutive ratings from the same person, posted 0.5–14 days apart, 51% matched exactly. The correlation was 0.63 and the standard deviation of the differences was 15.5 points. When we gave each of the 318 authors with a pair equal weight, about 47% matched exactly and 16% differed by at least 20 points.

Some people’s health may have changed between posts, and some matches may have picked up other uses of percentages. To measure how much Q4 varies on its own, we’ll need to ask patients twice about the same week and independently check that their health stayed stable between answers. Study sources v02_analysis/longit.py and next_version/audit.py.

Q4 asks how close people feel to their pre-illness selves. It is optional. Q5 asks how they’ve changed since the previous check.

Which changes would v0.2 have picked up?

The AI that designed the questionnaire screened 190 posts, split evenly between two severity groups based on keywords. Two other Sonnet reviewers divided the 157 posts it accepted. Without seeing its ratings, they identified 114 first-person accounts of change from 110 authors. Both sets of ratings below come from those same 114 accounts. The designer excluded 33 posts before the second review. Study sources v03_rechecks/c_coverage/CODEBOOK.md and v03_rechecks/c_coverage/analyze.py.

The blind reviewers judged that the questionnaire would fully capture the change in 57% of the accounts (95% interval 48–66%). The designer put that figure at 69% for the same accounts, a difference of 12.3 percentage points (95% interval 2.6–21.9). The intervals account for people who wrote more than one post and reflect variation across the selected accounts using the same reviewers. The two reviews agreed on 73 of 114 accounts (64%, kappa 0.29). Study sources v03_rechecks/c_coverage/analyze.py and next_version/audit.py.

The reviewers rated how much of each change the whole questionnaire might capture. They also marked each question they expected to pick up some aspect of the change. Out of 114 accounts, they marked Q3 in 100 (88%), Q5 in 91 (80%), Q4 in 65 (57%), Q2 in 63 (55%) and Q1 in 57 (50%).

In 22 accounts, reviewers marked the symptom question and left both the crash and activity questions unmarked. That included 17 of 60 accounts in the mild-to-moderate keyword group. This is one reason we’re keeping the symptom question for patient testing.

The blind reviewers’ notes show what the earlier draft could miss. Study source v03_rechecks/c_coverage/analyze.py.

  • Changes in individual symptoms. One overall score can hide which symptom changed, whether it was heart rate, breathing, pain, sleep, smell, or mental health.
  • Flares triggered by infection, treatment, or other non-exertional causes fell outside the v0.2 crash question. The v0.3.1 question counts crash or flare days from any trigger.
  • Changes that no question picked up. Reviewers judged that v0.2 would miss 11 of 114 changes entirely, including isolated changes in smell and new neuropathy.

In the 157 posts it accepted, the designer also found cases where people crashed after an activity or described a change that took longer than a week. In patient interviews, we’ll test response options for people who did an activity and then crashed, skipped it for health reasons, or had no opportunity to do it. We’ll also test a question about one symptom the patient considers important. Study source v02_analysis/coverage/analyze_cov.py.

What the trial literature adds

  • RECOVER-NEURO enrolled 328 adults and found no differential benefit from the tested interventions on its modified ECog2 primary outcome. The contribution of outcome measurement to that result remains open. RECOVER-NEURO trial.
  • A registry analysis reported PEM as a primary or co-primary endpoint in 8 of 714 registered Long COVID trials. Gabriel et al. preprint.
  • In interviews with 30 adults with Long COVID, participants understood most RECOVER questionnaire items and suggested ways to shorten or simplify them. Interviewers observed cognitive burden. RECOVER cognitive interviews.
  • The modified Rankin Scale has been used as an ordinal primary outcome in stroke trials. A trial substudy quantified variability between raters. Stroke trial substudy.
  • A survey of 3,925 patients reported mean pre-illness capacity ratings of 41.0% for ME/CFS and 50.6% for Long COVID among those with capacity data. The survey used descriptive capacity categories. Eckey et al. survey.

Limitations

  • Reddit over-represents people who are well enough to post and whose health has changed. Very severe patients are under-represented.
  • Severity tiers came from an LLM extraction that merges each patient's posts without dates, which blurs before-and-after.
  • All coding was done by AI models, not people, and human double-coding is still needed. Agreement varies by field:
    • Sonnet and DeepSeek agreed with Cohen's kappa 0.61–0.84 on 68 posts, all from the improvement-title sample. Study source v02_analysis/analyze_coded.py.
    • Two Sonnet coders agreed with kappa 0.54–0.87 on 40 posts. The lowest field was current severity (0.54). Study source v02_analysis/quality/agree.py.
    • Agreement on which change mattered most was kappa 0.46 on 20 posts. See coding priorities.
    • Agreement on v0.2 coverage was kappa 0.29 on 114 posts. See coverage exercise.
    One coding batch filled skipped fields with defaults; excluding it changed headline figures by 5 points or less.
  • The posts were chosen by titles saying "better" or "worse", so they can't test whether activity and symptoms move in opposite directions within a person.
  • No item has been formally tested for test-retest reliability, responsiveness or minimal important difference. The repeat ratings above are only an indirect look at Q4.
  • Q2 counts only activities from at least two days before the check, so in effect it covers five days. Whether this cuts recall error or just shrinks the window is untested.
  • The win-ratio margin (1 crash day a week) and the responder thresholds are placeholders until the minimal important difference has been measured.
  • Q2 records what people did, not what they could have done. Someone who avoids restaurants because of infection risk, or has no job to go back to, scores lower without being less well. The wording reduces this but doesn't remove it; interviews need to test a "didn't try, for other reasons" option.
  • The order of the 13 items in the top two groups, whether people at the top hit the ceiling, and whether the four new floor items work for very severe patients and their carers are all untested.
  • Proxy answers reflect a carer's observations and need their own validation. Assisted self-reports contain the patient's answers.

Validation plan

The next-version design and validation plan documents the audit, candidate wording and prospective comparisons.

  • Concept check: a dated, post-level extraction of change statements across the corpus, with human double-coding of 500 statements.
  • Patient interviews: 20–30 patients across severity levels, including bedbound patients by voice or proxy, with patient-led organisations as partners. Test the revised activity response options and a separate item for a symptom the patient names as important.
  • Prospective cohort: 300–500 patients, weekly for 12 weeks, alongside SF-36 physical function, FUNCAP27, the DePaul PEM questions and a step tracker subset. Measure reliability in stable weeks, responsiveness, minimal important change, and floor and ceiling effects. Compare how well Q2 and Q3 pick up change, and use the cohort to set the win-ratio margin and the responder thresholds.
  • Diary bridge: compare weekly crash days with daily entries and test five- and seven-day activity windows with 48- and 72-hour follow-up.
  • Trial use: run it as an exploratory endpoint in a live trial before proposing it as a primary.
