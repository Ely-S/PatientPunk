Counting crashes and tracking everyday activity

PatientPunk · draft for discussion

A walk can feel fine while you’re out and leave you worse the next day. You might also have fewer symptoms in a week when you’ve had to give up more of what you usually do. Weekly Check is a short questionnaire for people with Long COVID or ME/CFS that asks about crash days, everyday activities, and symptoms together.

In interviews about the questionnaires used by RECOVER, people with Long COVID suggested shorter forms and simpler wording. To see what a short check should ask about, we searched 3.8 million posts and comments from 15 Reddit communities going back to 2009. AI reviewers then read several hundred selected posts to identify what people said had changed. Trial literature

A crash can start the next day

Across 3,845,980 posts and comments, searches found about 104,000 texts with crash-related words and about 140,000 that mentioned post-exertional malaise (PEM) or related wording. Terms like bedbound appeared in about 50,000 texts and baseline in about 35,000. Searches for SF-36, PROMIS and FUNCAP found 89, 27 and 364 texts, respectively. These counts helped us choose familiar words for the quiz. Vocabulary search

The search for timing near a crash matched 6,125 texts, including about 3,500 with next-day wording. Some matches described how long a crash lasted. The accounts of delayed worsening helped shape two questions. One counts days spent in a crash or flare, whatever set it off. The other asks about activities from at least two days ago to give a delayed crash time to appear. We’ll compare the cutoff with daily diaries. Crash-delay search

What happens after an activity matters

Getting through a task and feeling well enough afterwards to carry on with your day are two different experiences. We found 6,140 phrases about doing things without a later crash or PEM. That helped shape a checklist of what people actually did without a crash afterwards. The list runs from getting to the toilet independently to walking farther or managing a full day of work or study. What people get a chance to do also affects their score. Activity analysis

A specific symptom was the most common change reviewers identified

In 104 of 242 selected posts (43%), AI reviewers chose a specific symptom as the main change. They chose the course of the illness in 56 (23%), feeling like oneself in 23 (10%), and activity and crashes in 20 each (8%). Reviewers agreed on 12 of the 20 posts they both read. Coding results

In 41 of the 242 posts (17%), reviewers saw symptoms ease or hold steady as people did less, or worsen as they did more. That’s one reason Weekly Check reports symptoms and activities side by side. Coding results

What Weekly Check asks

  • Crash days. How many of the past seven days did you spend in a crash or flare?
  • Activities without a later crash. Which of the 23 activities did you do this week without a crash afterwards? Count activities from at least two days ago.
  • Symptoms. How bad were your symptoms on a typical day this week, on a scale from 0 to 10?
  • Feeling like yourself. How close did you feel to your pre-illness self, from 0% to 100%? This question is optional.
  • Change since the last check. How are you doing overall compared with your last check? Choose “No earlier check” if this is your first.
  • In your own words. What changed this week, if anything? This question is optional.

A carer can enter your answers or give a separate report based on what they’ve observed. Each of the four activity groups has “All of these” and “None of these” buttons. You’ll see an activity total once you’ve answered all four groups. Scoring rules

For trials, one proposal compares how much crash days changed for a treated participant and a control participant. If their changes differ by at least one crash day per week, crash days decide the comparison. If the difference is smaller, the change in activities decides it. Patient feedback and weekly data will help test that rule. Proposed trial scoring

What changed after the first drafts

Our first draft leaned on asking people how close they felt to their pre-illness selves. That wording appeared more often in posts about recovery or improvement, and the ratings varied across posts by the same person. We made the question optional. We also replaced a single activity ladder with a checklist because many activities had similar difficulty estimates. Self-rating analysis · Activity analysis

The AI that designed v0.2 screened 190 posts. Two other AI reviewers looked at the 157 posts that passed that screen. Without seeing the designer’s ratings, they identified 114 first-person accounts of change. The reviewers judged that v0.2 would fully capture the change in 65 of those accounts (57%). The designer gave that rating to 79 of the same accounts (69%). Reading the gaps helped us see where to revise the quiz and what to test in interviews. Coverage exercise

What we’ll test with patients

Forum posts come from people able to write about their health online. We still need to hear from patients across the severity range, including people who are too unwell to post. One question for interviews is whether asking about a symptom the patient chooses would pick up changes hidden by a single overall symptom score.

We’ll time the quiz with patients. In a 12-week study, we’ll check whether answers stay similar when someone’s health stays stable and whether scores move when their health changes. We’ll also collect existing questionnaire scores and step counts for comparison. After that, we plan to try it as an exploratory measure in a trial. Validation plan
